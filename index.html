From Chaos to Standard: Building a Dashboard Constructor

How we built a dashboard constructor, got a whole BI team to adopt it, and what it looked like a year later.

By Maria Lebedeva, BI Developer / BI Team Lead

Sooner or later, every BI team faces the same problem: dozens of dashboards, each built in its own style. In this article, I share how I led a data visualisation standardisation project — from spotting the problem and building a dashboard constructor to getting the team to adopt it and seeing the results a year later.

1. Why UX/UI matters in BI analytics

It's a topic that's close to my heart. I've worked on it in every company where I've been a BI analyst. And it's a real pain point even at the biggest companies.

I'm talking about UX/UI in the world of BI analytics.

So what does UX/UI have to do with dashboards? It's a foundation that many of my colleagues overlook — including experienced BI developers who've been in the field for years.

Here's the simple truth: a dashboard is your final product, the face of your work. And the first thing a user sees when opening a report isn't the numbers — it's the structure and the visual layer. Numbers come after.

Who are your "customers"? Colleagues, people from other departments. There can be hundreds, even thousands of them — and they're the ones shaping the UX of your product.

Imagine: your company has 500 reports across different departments. And there's a founder who's actively involved in the business and has access to every report your team builds.

If you work with a founder → they need a single, seamless product: consistent structure, clear design, familiar buttons, fonts and colours — no surprises. That's only possible when every report is built on shared standards.

Without that — imagine how frustrating it is for them to use your reports. And what that does to their trust in your work.

If you work with external partners → same idea, but with an extra layer: you're also signalling how mature your company's BI practice is.

In my experience, maybe one in ten BI analysts even thinks about this. Even fewer propose standardisation. And very few manage to actually implement it — in a way the rest of the team adopts.

So how do you get to solid, sustainable UX/UI across a company's dashboards? I led a data visualisation standardisation project at two companies — and below I'll walk through it step by step: from the idea to implementation and long-term support.

2. The idea: building the constructor

Over the years, I've landed on one guiding principle: simpler is better. It helps me build dashboards that make sense to the business — and leave behind a codebase that's actually readable.

But in practice, I keep seeing two extremes: either the visualisation potential is barely used, or the dashboard is so overloaded with every possible chart type that you can't make sense of it even after 5 minutes of staring at graphs and pie charts 😅

Another common pattern — inconsistent styling across dashboards within the same company (which means unpredictability for users), and noticeably slow build times.

At my last job, my colleagues and I didn't just build a report template — we went further and built a full dashboard constructor:

  • A single build style — colour palette, fonts, core buttons
  • A shared calendar and reference objects — to keep time-based analysis consistent across reports
  • 3 dashboard scenarios — overview page, standard page, long-read page
  • A design guide — where and when to use each colour or font
  • A visual guide — ready-made custom visuals for easy copying and scaling across client scenarios

What this solved right away:

  • Established a consistent brand style for the company
  • Improved usability for business users
  • Set a clear, predictable "skeleton" for every dashboard
  • Gave BI developers a simple, straightforward tool — building dashboards got easier
  • Sped up dashboard builds — they started shipping to production twice as fast

But that was just the first stage. Next came the interesting part: getting the whole team to actually adopt the new standard.

3. Adoption: getting the team to use it

Building a template is 20% of the work. The other 80% is getting the team to actually use it.

The foundation — a single reporting standard — was ready, but legacy reports and old habits weren't going anywhere. So my team and I had a long, occasionally painful process of fixing mistakes ahead of us 😅

What we did:

  • Ran workshops for BI developers
  • Tested the template from both sides — development and business users
  • Gathered feedback through surveys and made improvements
  • Introduced visual review — checking against the core visualisation principles

Then came the third, hardest stage: making the new standard stick. Making sure old (legacy) reports gradually migrated to the new template, and new reports were built to the new standard from day one.

This is where we introduced report review as an ongoing practice — specifically visual review, checking the same core principles we'd set from the start.

As team lead, I got personally involved: I ran individual sessions with each BI developer in a "let's walk through this together" format, answered every question, and made sure they understood which details actually mattered.

After that, I set up a rotation of on-duty reviewers — developers who'd shown a strong grasp of the new standards. This closed the gap on weak spots, got the team comfortable receiving feedback from peers, and gave others room to learn from mistakes.

The key insight: the template didn't limit creativity — it freed up time for it. Developers started experimenting more instead of spending energy on basic setup — another big win for the project.

4. A year later: results and impact

The project didn't just survive — it kept growing.

Over the past year, the dashboard standardisation project hasn't just stayed alive — it's kept evolving and paying off.

Within a year, we migrated 261 reports to the new reporting standard and released 32 new reports built in the new format from the start.

We've collected a lot of positive feedback from report users. And positive feedback from BI developers too — both those who were on the team before the project started, and those who joined much later, once we'd fully switched to building on the template and running visual reviews.

One thing worth highlighting: new joiners also noted how easy the template made dashboard building. It helps junior specialists just as much as people who joined us with experience in other BI tools.

All of this points to one conclusion: the project worked — and standardisation genuinely matters when building corporate reporting, especially once your company has 10+ dashboards.

Conclusion

Standardisation is not about uniformity for its own sake. When it's thoughtfully designed and paired with training and review, a dashboard constructor becomes an enabler: it creates a consistent user experience, speeds up delivery and gives developers more room to focus on meaningful visualisation work.

If your report portfolio feels chaotic, a small set of shared standards and a simple review process can transform how analytics is perceived across the organisation.
